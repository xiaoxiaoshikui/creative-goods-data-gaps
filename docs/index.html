Who is missing from creative economy statistics?

World exports of creative goods reached about US$709 billion in 2024. But the economies the creative-economy agenda most wants to support are the ones least visible in the data. This dashboard maps both the market and the gaps.

Yunxiang Guo · November 2026 · Data: UNCTADstat (creative goods, 2002–2024), UN M49 · Code and data on GitHub

01The market has shifted to developing economies, mostly to China

05The definition itself moves every five years

UNCTAD defines creative goods as a list of 6-digit Harmonized System (HS) codes. The World Customs Organization revises the HS about every five years, so the list has to be re-mapped each time. The chart counts how each creative code from one edition maps to the next, using the UN Statistics Division correlation tables. Any code that is not kept one-to-one is a point where a time series can break.

06What this means for creative-economy measurement

  1. Read aggregates for small and least developed economies with care. In UNCTADstat the LDC total is flagged as estimated for every year it is published, and it is missing altogether for 2002, 2003 and 2024. Changes over time can reflect who reported rather than what was traded.
  2. Mirror data can close part of the gap. When an economy does not report, its partners' imports from it are usually still recorded. A partner-reported series for non-reporting economies, flagged as such, would put many of the grey economies back on the map.
  3. Publish the concordance with the indicator. Video games, recorded media and toys were entirely re-mapped in HS 2022, and UNCTAD moved works of art over 100 years old from visual arts to cultural heritage. A code-level concordance published alongside each release would let users see which breaks in a series come from the classification rather than from trade.
  4. Goods are only part of the creative economy. Crafts and design dominate goods trade, while music, audiovisual and publishing increasingly move as services and digital flows. Indicators of the effects of AI and digital platforms will need sources beyond customs data, such as platform and marketplace data, with the same attention to coverage.

07Method and sources

Trade data. UNCTADstat, “Creative goods: value” (US.CreativeGoodsValue), bulk download accessed 18 November 2026. Exports to the world, US$ at current prices, 2002–2024. UNCTAD defines creative goods as a list of Harmonized System product codes grouped into seven categories, with crafts and design split into seven sub-groups.

Groups. UN Statistics Division, Standard country or area codes (M49), for region and for LDC, LLDC and SIDS membership. Development-status totals (developed, developing, developing excluding China) are UNCTADstat's own aggregates.

Coverage measure. An economy has a gap in a year when UNCTADstat lists it but publishes no export value. Historical entities that no longer exist (for example Netherlands Antilles, Serbia and Montenegro) are excluded. Economies created after 2002 (Curaçao, Sint Maarten, South Sudan, Serbia, Montenegro, Bonaire, Sint Eustatius and Saba) are counted from their first year. 2024 is shown separately because reporting for the latest year is still incomplete. A missing value means that no figure is published. It does not mean that the economy had no creative trade.

HS revisions. UNCTADstat, creative product groupings for HS 2002, 2007, 2012, 2017 and 2022, matched against the UNSD HS correlation tables. Each creative code is classed as kept, renumbered, split (1:n), merged (n:1) or complex (n:m). The count is of codes, not trade value. Many HS 2022 “complex” cases are small partial links to new headings for e-waste (85.49) and display modules (85.24), and in most of them the original code still exists.
